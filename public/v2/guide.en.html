Before you start, get these few things straight

What a model is

A model is a file several GB in size. What it holds isn't code — it's the skill of "having looked at millions of drawings" and learned how to draw.

It doesn't think, and it doesn't understand what you're saying. It does exactly one thing: continue the picture.

You give it a paragraph, it draws an image that fits that paragraph as closely as it can. Write something vague and it makes things up — when that works out, that's luck; usually it doesn't.

The model decides style and skill. Same person, same prompt, different model — it looks like a different artist drew it.

What a LoRA is

A LoRA is a small file, tens to hundreds of MB, that adds one specific lesson.

The base model doesn't know who "Tribbie" is, and it doesn't know a particular art style. Attach the matching LoRA and it recognises it.

So what a LoRA does is add one specialist class to a general-purpose artist: how this face is drawn, how this hairstyle is drawn, how this outfit is drawn.

A LoRA can't be used alone; it always hangs off a model.

There's also the trap beginners fall into most often: a LoRA and a model have to match. Put an A-series LoRA on a B-series model and most of the time it does nothing — no error, images still come out, but the LoRA never took effect and you're seeing whatever the model improvised.

(The "Health check" in this app looks at this. When it reports a mismatched LoRA, swap that one out.)

What a prompt is

A prompt has two fields: positive and negative.

Positive says what you want.

1girl, white shirt, black skirt, classroom, full body

Negative says what you don't want.

lowres, bad hands, extra fingers, watermark

One line to remember the difference: positive is the menu, negative is the allergies.

Why you always need negative words

Because the model has no idea what "drawn badly" means.

It has no hands, and it has never seen how many joints a real finger has. It only knows "probability says there should be a line here", so a hand with six fingers doesn't look wrong to it.

Negative words work like this: tell it in advance where things usually go wrong, so it leans careful there.

Don't write bad hands and it won't care about hands; write it and it gets extra careful while drawing them.

There's a second job too: whatever you leave out, it fills in itself.

Write only "a person" with no background and it may give you a blank white sheet, or a forest. Anything you don't want it inventing, write it yourself.

Beginners should start with this negative set — it saves about half your bad images:

lowres, worst quality, bad anatomy, bad hands, bad feet,
extra fingers, missing fingers, watermark, text, signature

Don't copy two hundred of them. Overloading the negative flattens the image, greys out the colour and stiffens the pose.

How to write the positive prompt

Word order is priority

A prompt is read left to right. The earlier a word is, the more weight the model gives it.

The recommended order:

who → doing what → wearing what → where → how it's shot → light → quality

You don't have to fill every slot. Any slot you leave empty, the model fills for you.

1. Say who it is first

Start with how many people are in frame.

1girl one girl, 2girls two, 1boy one boy. Without a count, the model may draw three.

Then the person: hair, eyes, build.

long hair, black hair, blue eyes, twintails

One word deserves its own note: solo. It means "only this one person in frame". Without it, the model often squeezes a passer-by in, or pushes a hand into the frame.

2. What they're doing

This is what beginners skip most often, and it affects the final image the most.

standing      standing
sitting       sitting
lying on bed  lying on a bed
kneeling      kneeling
leaning       leaning
walking       walking

Same character, same outfit, change only this line, and the feel changes completely.

Write just standing and you get a character sheet — person standing, background blurred out, like an ID photo.

Write standing by the window, looking back and you get a picture — there's action, direction, a story.

The first is "what this character looks like", the second is "what this person is doing". You decide which one you want.

3. What they're wearing

Name the clothes one piece at a time, and split top from bottom.

white shirt, black skirt, thighhighs, brown shoes

Write just "school uniform" and the model may hand you a swimsuit — it only knows "school uniform" is roughly a school thing.

4. Where

Start with the broad stroke: indoors, outdoors.

Then narrow down:

classroom   classroom
bedroom     bedroom
beach       beach
forest      forest
street      street

Three scene words are enough. Write ten and the model no longer knows where you wanted to be, and gives you mush.

5. How it's shot

This group is the camera. It decides whether the image is a character sheet or a photograph.

full body     whole body
upper body    upper half
cowboy shot   above the thighs
portrait      face only
from behind   shot from behind
from side     side view
from above    looking down
from below    looking up

Same outfit, same action, change only this line:

  • full body → calm, you see the whole person, but no presence
  • from below → looking up, the person feels tall and imposing
  • from above → looking down, the person feels small and vulnerable
  • from behind → no face, the posture and back tell the story

If a beginner learns only one thing, learn this. Changing the camera angle does more than changing the outfit.

6. Light

backlight       rim light, edges glowing
moonlight       cold, night
soft lighting   soft, clean face
dramatic light  hard, high contrast

Light is the door between "readable" and "good looking". Add backlight to an image and it's immediately different.

One rule for beginners: light needs a direction. light from the window beats good lighting a hundred times over.

7. Quality words

masterpiece, best quality, highly detailed

These three are widely recognised by models now, and they do make it a bit more refined.

But don't expect them to save an image with a broken composition. Quality words are decoration, not foundation.

How to use weights

The syntax is (word:number). Above 1 strengthens, below 1 weakens.

(blush:1.2)      blush shows more
(smile:0.8)      smile softer

When to raise a weight

  • Something you want isn't showing up (you wrote blush but the face stays pale) → raise to 1.2–1.3
  • Something you don't want keeps appearing (always draws large breasts) → push it down, or add it to the negative
  • Two words fight each other (e.g. smile and sad together) → pick one, delete the other

How much

1.1 to 1.3 is normally enough. Above 1.5 it usually breaks.

The most common beginner mistake is cranking everything to 1.5 or 2.0. Overshoot burns the image: hair fuses into the face, colours go scorched, lines get hard.

What if raising doesn't work

If a word still won't appear at 1.4, it isn't a weight problem — the model simply doesn't know that word, or it conflicts with something else.

More weight won't help then. Change the word, or delete the conflicting one.

How to pair LoRAs

Where it goes

On top of the model you picked. One character LoRA + one base model is the most basic setup.

What weight

LoRA weight is a separate thing from prompt weight, but the syntax looks the same.

  • 0.6 to 0.8 is the usual range — the balance between character fidelity and the model's style
  • Too low (below 0.5) the character doesn't look like herself, she drifts to the model's default face
  • Too high (above 0.9) she looks right but turns stiff and the background collapses — because the LoRA was trained mostly on standing close-ups, it doesn't know how to draw backgrounds

Beginners start at 0.7, add 0.05 if it doesn't look like her, subtract 0.05 if it looks stiff.

Don't stack too many

One character + one outfit LoRA is the limit a beginner can hold steady.

Three or more stacked together pollute each other's colours (moonlight + a red character → she turns purple), and hands and feet smudge.

Attach one first, get the person right. Then think about adding more.

Don't let LoRAs fight

Two LoRAs both changing the same area (both editing the face, say) will fight, and the result is two faces smeared together.

If you really need both, push the less important one down (0.4–0.5).

One complete run, start to finish

Target: a black long-haired girl, white shirt and black skirt, standing in a classroom at dusk, full body.

Step 1, pick the model. Choose an anime base model.

Step 2, attach the character LoRA. Weight 0.7.

Step 3, write the positive prompt.

1girl, solo, long black hair, blue eyes,
white shirt, black skirt, thighhighs,
standing, classroom, sunset, long shadows,
full body, backlight

Count them: eighteen words. That's the length a beginner should be at.

Step 4, write the negative prompt.

lowres, worst quality, bad anatomy, bad hands, bad feet,
extra fingers, missing fingers, watermark, text, signature

Step 5, generate. Look at what's wrong.

Say the problem is: the face is fine, but the background is a blur, you can't tell it's a classroom.

Step 6, change one thing.

Move classroom earlier, or turn it into (classroom:1.2).

Change only that one thing, run again. Compare.

If it improves, it was a weight problem; if nothing changes, the model responds weakly to that word and you need something more specific, like desks, chalkboard, classroom.

That's the whole loop. One change at a time, then look at the difference.

The three mistakes beginners make most

1. Too many words

Sixty words, and the image looks like nothing.

Model attention is finite; the more words, the less each one gets. Twenty words or fewer is the comfortable range for beginners.

Rather write ten fewer words than ten useless ones.

2. Saying the same thing twice

smile, happy, grinning together and the model thinks you want a face laughing itself apart.

long hair, very long hair, floor length hair and the hair grows to the floor.

Pick the one that's most accurate.

3. Not knowing how to change it

When the image is wrong, don't rewrite the whole line. If you rewrite everything, you don't know which phrase did what.

Change one thing. Look at the difference immediately. Ten runs like that and you know what each word does.

How to practise

Don't chase perfection from the start.

Round 1, write twelve words only.

1girl, solo, standing, white shirt, black skirt, classroom, full body

Run one, see what you get.

Round 2, add an expression. Add smile, see if the face changes.

Round 3, change the camera. Swap full body for from below, see if the presence changes.

Round 4, add light. Add backlight or moonlight, see if the mood changes.

Round 5, attach a LoRA. Pick a character, weight 0.7, see if she becomes that person.

Five rounds and you know what the words in each slot are doing. From then on, when you see someone else's image you can roughly guess how they wrote it.

Last line

Words are tools, not spells.

The more you write like you're telling a person "I'd like to see this one frame", the closer it gets.
